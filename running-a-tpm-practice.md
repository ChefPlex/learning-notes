# Running a TPM Practice

**Notes from leading the technical program management practice at Taos, a consulting firm, from 2011 to 2018.**

Companion piece: [Building the TPM Function](https://github.com/ChefPlex/tpm-templates/blob/main/building-the-tpm-function.md)
in tpm-templates. That one is about hiring and placing your first TPMs inside a company. This one is
about leading a group of them from a consulting firm, where none of them worked in your office.

---

## The shape of the job

The practice was 15+ senior consultant TPMs, placed at client sites. Some sites had several of
them, and at some of those sites I was leading the work myself. I owned their annual reviews, their
compensation recommendations and the escalation path.

Two goals ran side by side the whole time. We were trying to grow the practice at each site, and we
were trying to make each customer very happy. Most of what follows serves one or the other, and
usually both.

## Hiring

Interviews were by phone and video, and some weeks I did five, six or seven of them.

My main question was simple: what model do you use to run a program? Every model has a start, a
middle and an end, so the interesting part is what the candidate brings up along the way.

- **The start.** Do they talk about documentation, a business case, a charter?
- **The middle.** How do they get from the start to doing the work? How do they handle the design,
  get resources and negotiate priorities?
- **The plan.** How do they build a timeline, and what do they base it on? What are the milestones?
- **The end.** How do they finish it off?

A lot of that is theory, so I asked for their own examples as we went.

Two things happen in every interview. You know in the first five minutes whether you want to hire
the person. That is the gut. The rest of the interview is for finding out
how much they actually know and whether they fit the job. The gut is not enough without that part.

We hired a very small share of the people I talked to. I don't remember the rate, but it was low,
and we were getting good TPMs.

## Landing at the site

A new hire usually filled an open rec at a specific client site, and often it was a site I was
already leading. So the first job was bringing them up to speed: how we work there, who the
important people are, how the work actually gets done, and what is expected of them.

## Keeping a practice together across sites

The practice was spread across client sites, so holding it together took meetings on purpose.

**At each site,** I held periodic meetings of our TPMs: what we had learned, what was not going
well, and what problems were coming. I kept track of who was doing what and how they were doing,
and I met with senior management as well.

**Across all sites,** once a month, everyone met. One of the TPMs or I presented a topic, such as
risk, or constraints: there are the three main ones everyone learns, and many others that turn up on
real programs. Then we talked it through. I bought dinner, so people could sit around a table
together and get to know each other.

## When a placement went wrong

Sometimes the feedback was that a placement was not going well and the customer was not happy. My
order was always the same.

1. **The customer first.** I went out and talked to them, to see what was working and what was not.
2. **Then the TPM.** I met with them.
3. **Then a plan, made together,** to get them back to being the people the customer was glad to
   have.

## Where this stops

I don't have a hire rate, and I won't guess one. I don't have a specific case of a placement that
went wrong that I remember well enough to write down, so the method above is the method, not a story.

A consulting practice is also a particular kind of leadership. The client set each TPM's daily work.
My levers were hiring, landing people well, keeping the practice connected, and stepping in when a
placement went wrong.
